Erik Ulfsson took his first command at sixteen, against raiders who had come ashore on the Jylland coast in the summer of 1078. The record of that engagement is brief: he led, the raiders were driven off, and his father Ulf — called Sea-Wolf — had set him at the watch as much for the testing as for the need. Whether the boy distinguished himself or merely held the line, no chronicle now says. What is certain is that two summers later he was thought fit to be married, and Astrid, daughter of a neighbouring jarl, was brought to his hall.

The first quiet years of that marriage are unattested, as the quiet years of most lives are. They end abruptly in November of 1085, when Sea-Wolf's death brought Erik the lordship of Jylland and, within the same week, the injury that would mark him for the rest of his life. The funeral games for a Norse jarl were not gentle work; in one of their rounds Erik broke his leg badly. It set wrong. He walked thereafter with a heavy limp, and within the year his men had ceased to call him Ulfsson alone. He was Erik the Lame, and he answered to it.

A man with that name and that gait might have stayed close to his hearth. Erik did not. In the spring of 1095 he took his ships west and joined Saemundr the Wise, the Iceland chieftain, in the war over that island. The campaign ran seven months and ended in defeat, recorded plainly and not embellished. He came home with fewer men than he had taken. What it cost him in standing or in silver, the record does not say; only that the war ended, and that he kept his hall.

The years that followed at last brought him a surviving son. Hákon was born in the autumn of 1102, when his father was forty; the child lived through the dangerous early seasons, and that was the matter that mattered most in a Norse hall. Erik would have nearly six more years to teach the boy what a lame jarl knows about ruling: that the leg matters less than the listening, and that a defeat carried home is itself a kind of teaching.

He died of fever in April of 1108, in his forty-sixth year, and was buried at Aalborg. Hákon, not yet six, would in time take the hall his father had held.
